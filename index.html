01 · PROCESS

From MatrAIx personas to questionnaire responses

We started with the MatrAIx persona dataset, aligned demographic and household backgrounds with public data, updated health and treatment details, and then asked all 900 personas the same questionnaire.

  1. 01

    Set up the nine comparison groups

    Cross North, Central and South India with ages 18–30, 31–50 and 51–75. Each group contains 100 personas. Within those groups, use Census 2011 data to set age, state, rural or urban residence and sex as recorded in the source.

    Result: 900 personas across 15 states or union territories, including 609 rural and 291 urban residents.

  2. 02

    Give each persona a household and resource context

    Match Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES) 2023–24 records by state, rural or urban residence, age group and recorded sex. Take monthly spending per person, household size and education from the same household record so these details stay together. Select the sample to match reference spending distributions within each of the nine groups.

    Result: Each persona has a household size, education background and a local spending position, spanning lower, middle and higher consumption levels.

  3. 03

    Retain the rest of the persona’s daily-life background

    Keep the matched MatrAIx persona’s employment, housing, available routine and commuting details, and physical function—including mobility, vision, hearing, dexterity and pain.

    Result: The demographic and resource profile sits within a detailed everyday-life context.

  4. 04

    Update specific diagnosed conditions

    Use published Indian survey rates to assign previously diagnosed hypertension, diabetes and cardiovascular history within the ages covered by those sources. For diabetes, use finer age bands and estimate the rural–urban and sex breakdown from the available tables. Update conflicting health descriptions—for example, remove “no chronic condition” when a chronic diagnosis has been assigned.

    Result: The full cohort contains 91 personas with hypertension, 65 with diabetes and 28 with cardiovascular history. A persona can have more than one condition.

  5. 05

    Rewrite treatment details to match the updated health background

    Generate medication and non-drug treatment arrangements that fit each persona’s conditions. Record what was recommended, which condition it relates to, and the relevant treatment period. Then ask the questionnaire whether the persona obtained treatment, started it, followed it or stopped.

    Result: Health conditions and treatment recommendations form the starting background; care-seeking, medicine-taking and record-keeping are collected as answers.

Population: Census 2011 · Household resources: HCES 2023–24 · Health: NNMS 2017–18 and published diabetes age tables.

02 · MEASUREMENT

Eight areas, including two named scales

The questionnaire covers eight measurement areas, organized below. GAMQ and SMAQ are the two named scales within this wider questionnaire.

In total, there are 41 study-authored question blocks, a 12-item GAMQ, a 6-item SMAQ, and one medication eligibility check. Together these make 44 top-level questionnaire steps; follow-up questions depend on the persona’s answers.

01

Seeking care

What prompts help-seeking, where people turn first, barriers, and the timing of help for a recent health problem.

02

Views about illness

How important professional help seems, comfort discussing illness, and reasons for these views for communicable and noncommunicable conditions.

03

Attitudes to medicines

General trust, concerns and reluctance, plus expected benefits and hesitation about a personally relevant medicine.

04

Taking medicines

Prescribed arrangements, obtaining and starting medicines, ongoing use, and reported missed doses.

05

Following non-drug advice

Recommendations such as exercise or other non-drug care, whether they were started, and how they were followed.

06

Other health practices and beliefs

Home remedies, traditional services and spiritual practices: use, reasons, perceived benefits and relation to professional care.

07

Family and social influence

Whose views matter, practical support, expectations, pressure and reported influence on health choices.

08

Records and health timelines

Receiving, storing and retrieving records, and how health events and their timing are remembered.

How to read the two named scales

GAMQ · 12 ITEMS

Attitudes to medicines

The General Attitude towards Medication Questionnaire measures trust, concerns and reluctance to take medicines. The total ranges from 12 to 60, with higher totals indicating a more positive overall attitude.

Trust ranges from 4–20; concerns from 3–15; reluctance from 5–25. Each subscore increases with the strength of that tendency. Scores are reported as a mean and standard deviation, which describes variation between personas.

Peerdeman, Tekampe et al. (2024) · CC BY 4.0
SMAQ · 6 ITEMS

Reported medication-taking

The Simplified Medication Adherence Questionnaire asks about forgetting, carelessness, stopping when feeling worse, and missed doses over the last week, last weekend and three months.

Its published rules classify adherence from the pattern of answers, rather than adding the six items into a total. We show individual responses and, where all required information is available, the complete classification.

Knobel et al. (2002)

The study was administered in English. The findings below focus on access to care, differences between groups, continuity of care, medication-taking and family involvement.

03 · FINDINGS

Follow the journey from seeking help to managing care

The main pattern is a practical one: cost and time feature strongly when people consider seeking help; distance varies sharply by living context; and difficulties finding records continue after contact with care. Medication responses and family involvement then show what day-to-day support might need to address.

3.1

First, reaching care has a practical cost

Access barriers / first source of help ·

Across all 900 personas, cost is selected by 780 (86.7%) and time pressures by 691 (76.8%). A health professional is the usual first source of help for 332 (36.9%); 307 (34.1%) first turn to family or friends. Together, these answers suggest that making professional help easier to fit into everyday life is a central service opportunity.

3.3

The journey continues through waiting and record retrieval

For the most recent health problem, 463/875 (52.9%) report seeking professional help after 8–30 days, while 194/875 (22.2%) have not yet sought it. After contact with care, continuity is another issue: 447/521 have records for only some visits, and 459/521 have been unable to retrieve medical information when needed. The service opportunity extends from arranging help to keeping information usable across visits.

3.4

Day-to-day treatment support needs a closer look

The average GAMQ score is 39.19 out of a possible 12–60. Among the 234 personas eligible for SMAQ, all report sometimes forgetting medicine and 169 report occasional carelessness. All 212 with complete classification fall into the nonadherent category, so the individual answers are more informative here than a comparison of adherence rates. Forgetting and routines are useful topics for follow-up service research.

3.5

Care also sits within existing practices and conversations

Home remedies are reported by 866/900 and spiritual or religious practices by 883/900. No persona reports traditional service use. When discussing illness with family or friends, the most common response is “moderately comfortable” for communicable conditions (736/900), and “very comfortable” for noncommunicable conditions (662/900). These findings bring everyday practices and communication preferences into the care journey.

3.6

Health decisions often involve family

Family involvement connects several stages of that journey: advice before a decision, reminders during treatment, and practical help reaching services. The question for service design is how to support these roles while letting the individual choose who participates.

Support extends beyond accompanying someone to care

898/900 report discussing their situation or receiving advice from family or friends; 394/900 report reminders about medicines, exercises or follow-up visits; and 206/900 report help with services, transport or accompaniment. 403/900 describe a large influence from family and friends, and 497/900 a moderate influence.

04 · IMPLICATIONS

Design around a connected care journey

Taken together, the findings suggest a service that helps people take the next step, carry their information forward, and manage care between visits. The priorities below connect each design direction to the results behind it.

1. Make the next care step easier to take

Evidence: Cost and time are the most frequently selected barriers, while the rural–urban comparison highlights transport. Family and friends are almost as common a first source of help as professionals.

Design direction: Combine clear fee information, help finding and booking an appropriate service, and appointment options that fit work, caregiving and travel.

2. Keep information connected across visits

Evidence: 447/521 have records for only some encounters, and 459/521 have needed information they could not retrieve.

Design direction: Make it easy to capture paper records, organize them into a health timeline and find the right document at the next visit. Include assistance from a chosen family member where wanted.

3. Fit ongoing support into daily routines

Evidence: Forgetting medicine is common in the eligible responses; 394/900 also describe reminders from family or friends. Home remedies and spiritual practices form part of many personas’ daily health context.

Design direction: Explore routine-based medication support and optional family assistance alongside the person’s existing ways of managing health.
